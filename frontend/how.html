Why “how sure”?

Many different models fit the same data about equally well. They can still give one person different answers.

GLIMPSE shows the whole range of those answers, so a close call looks like a close call.

Researchers call this group of equally good models the Rashomon set. Most tools that suggest changes (“counterfactual explanations”) look at one model from it. GLIMPSE takes the whole set into account: the range bars, the safe change, and “what drove it” all cover every good model.

Reading the map

The map squeezes everyone onto a flat page. People with similar numbers sit close together.

Background colour
The simple model's chance at each spot: one colour for each answer, pale near 50%. Other colour sets are under My account.
Shading
Where people are. The darkest band holds the most crowded 20% of people. Each lighter band adds 20% more, and the outer line holds 80%. No single person is shown.
Model's line
Where the model's answer flips.
Other good models
Thin lines: models that fit the data about as well as the main one.
Strictest model
The good model least on your side. Past its line, every good model agrees.
★ ✕ ◆ ▪
You, the smallest change, the change with fewest values, and the safest change. The arrow points to the safest one.
Click anywhere
Shows the estimated person who would sit at that spot.
Around this person
Rebuilds each map around the star, so both suggested changes show at their true distance and direction. Other people are shown by how they differ from you along those two directions.

Close call or clear

Close call: some good models say one answer and some say the other.

Clear: every good model gives the same answer.

The range bar shows it: the dot is the best guess, the band is what the other good models say.

What drove it

Each bar shows how much one of your values pushes the model toward one answer, compared with an average person. The biggest pushes are listed first.

The thin line shows what the other good models say. When it crosses the middle, good models disagree on which way that value pushes.

Three ways to change it

Smallest change (✕): the least total change that flips the main model's answer. It may nudge several values a little.

Fewest changes (◆): touches as few values as possible, often just one, even if that one moves further.

Safest change: the smallest change that flips every good model's answer. It is usually bigger.

Sometimes no safe change exists with the values you can change. GLIMPSE says so instead of showing one that doesn't work.

Suggested values always stay within what the data has seen.

What you can change

Under “What can you change?” on the map page, mark each value:

  • Can't change (lock), like family history.
  • Only up (↑) or only down (↓). Age and number of pregnancies are “only up” to start with.
  • Free to change.

Suggestions follow these choices. They are saved in this browser.

What the map can't show

A flat map can't show every number at once. Part of a change can happen in numbers the map doesn't draw.

So an arrow can look like it stops short of a line even when the change works. A note under the map says when this happens. The numbers in the cards are always exact.

Switch the map to Around this person to see the whole change: there, the map is built from your changes, so nothing is hidden.

The data

  • Diabetes: the Pima Indians Diabetes data (768 women, 8 health values).
  • Breast cancer: the Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer data (569 tumors, 30 measurements from scans).

Simple model: a straight-line model. Flexible model: a small neural network.

Your numbers

Nothing you type is stored unless you sign in and choose to save. You pick which values to keep, and you can delete them any time.
